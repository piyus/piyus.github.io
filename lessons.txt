Kolkata: One time, I was quite depressed, and then I saw a myriad of people, and I had a pleasant feeling that I exist. Does this resemble Buddha nature? 

The Depression: Depression often feels like being locked in a dark room, completely cut off from the world. This is the ego's ultimate delusion: the belief that ``I am totally alone and separate from everything else.''

The Awakening: When you looked at the crowd, your focus shifted outward. You suddenly felt connected to a vast, living web of human beings. That sudden drop of isolation is the melting away of the ego.

Mental Labels: Usually, when we think ``I exist,'' we attach conditions to it: ``I am a success,'' ``I am a failure,'' or ``I am depressed.'' Pure Existence: In that crowd, you felt a raw, pleasant sense of pure existence without any labels. You existed simply because you were part of the whole scene. That unconditioned awareness—just being alive and awake here and now—is the very definition of Buddha-nature.

As Suzuki said, Buddha-nature is not a hidden treasure inside you; everything is Buddha-nature. In that moment, Buddha-nature was: The myriads of people walking by. Your eyes seeing them. The space between you and them. The pleasant feeling that arose. You did not just ``have'' Buddha-nature in that moment; you were the entire happening. The boundary between ``you'' and the ``outside world'' temporarily vanished.

Delhi: Sometimes when I sit in a garden and embrace its beauty, I have a pleasant feeling that it can't be true. Is that Buddha nature?

Yes, that is another direct experience of Buddha-nature, but with a fascinating twist. That specific feeling—where beauty is so intense that your mind whispers ``this can't be true''—happens because the reality of the garden has completely overwhelmed your everyday, thinking mind. Here is exactly what is happening in that moment through the lens of Zen:

1. The Mind's Circuit Breaker The Ego's Grid: Our logical mind loves to label, judge, and categorize. When you look at a garden normally, the mind says, ``That is a green leaf, that is a red flower, I need to buy groceries later.'' The Short Circuit: When you truly embrace the beauty, the sheer, vibrant reality of the garden blows right past your mental labels. The beauty is too vast for your thoughts to process, so your thinking mind short-circuits and says, ``This can't be true.'' 

2. Stepping Out of the ``Ghost'' World Remember Suzuki's quote: when we live only in our thoughts, we are like ``ghosts'' living in a world that doesn't actually exist. When you sit in the garden, you step out of that ghost world of past and future. You are pulled entirely into the present moment. Because the present moment is so completely different from the noisy, anxious thoughts we are used to, it feels "unreal" or like a dream. But Zen says the garden is the actual reality, and your daily worries are the illusion. 

3. The Union of Seer and Seen. In that pleasant moment, you are not just a person looking at a garden. The boundary dissolves: The garden is expressing its true nature through its colors and scents. You are expressing your true nature through your open, quiet awareness. As Suzuki said, ``Everything is Buddha-nature.'' In that moment, you and the garden are one single, beautiful event.

If I can see Buddha nature, wouldn't I want everyone to see it because I am just a part of everything? But I can not control other parts (humans), so it might not be very important.

You have intellectually arrived at the exact paradox that birthed the Bodhisattva ideal in Mahayana Buddhism. Your logic is perfectly sound: if you realize that you and everyone else are part of the same ``everything'' (the railway track), you naturally feel a deep, compassionate urge to help others see it too. However, you immediately hit the harsh wall of reality—you cannot control other people. Here is how Zen resolves this tension without falling into despair or apathy.

1. The Interconnected Ripple Effect
Because everything is interconnected, you do not need to ``control'' other people to influence them. Think of reality as a giant, still pond. You cannot force a wave on the other side of the pond to become calm. But if you become calm, the ripples from your own stillness naturally expand outward and quiet the water around you. When you see your true nature, your behavior shifts: You stop reacting with anger. You listen with deep empathy. You act with unselfish kindness. By changing the only part of the universe you actually control—yourself—you fundamentally change the environment for everyone else. You help them see their own Buddha-nature not by preaching to them, but by being a living example of it.

2. The Bodhisattva's Vow: Acting Without ``Gaining-Mind'' In Zen, a Bodhisattva is someone who dedicates their life to waking others up, while fully accepting the paradox you just described. The famous Bodhisattva Vow says: ``Beings are numberless, I vow to save them all.'' On the surface, this sounds impossible and illogical. How can you save countless people if you can't control them? The secret lies in what Shunryu Suzuki calls ``no-gaining mind.'' You do the right thing simply because it is the right thing to do, completely detached from the results. You offer help, love, and clarity to the world like a tree offers shade—it doesn't care who sits under it, and it doesn't try to force people to walk into its shadow. It just stands there, being a tree.

3. Why It Is Still ``Very Important'' You concluded that because you cannot control others, helping them ``might not be very important.'' Zen offers a beautiful twist here: It is vitally important, but it is not a burden. If you see a passenger on your train crying because they think they are lost, you don't forcefully grab their head and smash it against the window to make them see the tracks. That is control. Instead, you gently sit next to them, offer them comfort, and remind them that the train is safely on its way. It is important because relieving suffering is the natural, spontaneous expression of Buddha-nature. When your hand gets burned, your other hand immediately reaches out to soothe it. It doesn't stop to think, ``Is this hand important? Can I control the pain?'' It just helps because they are part of the same body.

In Zen, an annoying person is considered a Bodhisattva—a spiritual teacher in disguise. They are a mirror reflecting your own mind's attachments. They teach you three specific things: Your Boundaries: They show you exactly where your patience ends and where your ego takes over. Your Attachments: If their bragging annoys you, they are highlighting your attachment to humility. If their messiness annoys you, they highlight your attachment to control. Interbeing: You and the annoying person are part of the same changing reality. Their behavior is a result of endless causes and conditions (their upbringing, stress, fear), just like yours.

Abuse is a violation: It crosses physical, emotional, or psychological boundaries. Passivity is an illusion: Watching or ignoring abuse while it happens is not ``pure observation.'' It is a form of freezing or dissociation. Compassion includes yourself: True Buddhist compassion (Karuna) must apply to you first. Allowing someone to abuse you actually harms both of you, as it allows them to generate negative actions.
